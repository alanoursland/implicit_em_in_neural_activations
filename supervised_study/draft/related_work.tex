\section{Related Work and Positioning}
\label{sec:related-work}
This paper asks why the EM-like optimizer dynamics observed in an isolated
implicit-EM model do not generally appear in composed neural networks, even
when those networks contain log-sum-exp or softmax interfaces. The relevant
prior work therefore falls into three groups: EM as an optimization method,
neural mechanisms that produce responsibility-shaped gradients, and training
methods that protect or balance local objectives under composition.

\subsection{EM as Optimization and Conditioning}
The expectation-maximization algorithm was introduced for maximum-likelihood
estimation with incomplete data \citep{dempster1977maximum}. Its classical
form alternates between computing posterior responsibilities and maximizing
an expected complete-data objective. The free-energy view of EM
\citep{neal1998view} makes this coordinate-ascent structure explicit and
clarifies why EM can be studied as an optimization procedure rather than only
as a probabilistic modeling algorithm.

The closest optimization precedent is the analysis of EM as a preconditioned
gradient method \citep{xu1996convergence}. That line of work treats EM's
behavior as a consequence of responsibility geometry and missing information.
This matters for the present paper because the decoder-free implicit-EM model
exhibited optimizer dynamics that are unusual for neural networks: SGD was
learning-rate insensitive over a wide range and adaptive optimization offered
little representational advantage. The question here is when a neural
responsibility-gradient site inherits enough of EM's optimization geometry to
produce those dynamics.

The local curvature constant used in our theory is not itself new. The
softmax or multinomial-logistic Hessian bound follows from the classical
bound of \citet{bohning1992multinomial}, and can also be obtained from an
elementary variance argument. The contribution here is to locate where that
bound applies in a neural objective and where it stops applying after learned
composition.

\subsection{Responsibility Gradients in Neural Networks}
Several neural architectures and objectives already contain
responsibility-like quantities. Mixtures of experts use learned soft gates
over expert heads and admit EM-style training
\citep{jordan1994hierarchical}. Attention mechanisms use softmax routing over
values \citep{vaswani2017attention}, and associative-memory interpretations
make the retrieval geometry explicit \citep{ramsauer2021hopfield}. More
recently, \citet{agarwal2025gradient} derived first-order attention training
dynamics in which cross-entropy induces advantage-form attention-score
updates and value updates weighted by attention responsibilities. They frame
the training-time EM correspondence as structural rather than variational:
the updates are driven by backpropagated error signals, not by an explicit
expected complete-data likelihood.

The implicit-EM identity gives a more general local statement for
log-sum-exp distance objectives: the gradient with respect to each component
distance is its posterior responsibility \citep{oursland2025implicitem}.
This addresses the training-time gap left by the structural analogy, under
the conditions of a distance or energy LSE site: the responsibility is not
only a useful routing interpretation but the gradient supplied to gradient
descent. This places unsupervised mixture-like objectives, supervised
cross-entropy, and attention-style softmax interfaces under a shared local
algebra. The present paper does not address inference-time Bayesian
computation in trained attention models. It asks the next training-time
question: when such responsibility-shaped gradients exist locally, do their
optimizer consequences survive the pullback through the rest of the network?

\subsection{The Single-Site Baseline}
The decoder-free sparse-autoencoder study tested the unsupervised branch of
the implicit-EM theory \citep{oursland2026decoderfree}. A single encoder with
an LSE objective and volume control learned structured prototype-like
features. The expected local failures appeared under ablation: without volume
control, components collapsed or died; with only anti-collapse control,
components remained redundant; with both variance and decorrelation, the
site remained active and diverse.

The more restrictive observation was the learning dynamics. In that isolated
single-site setting, SGD was insensitive to learning rate over several orders
of magnitude, Adam did not improve representation quality, and lower loss did
not necessarily produce better features. These are the properties whose
survival is tested here. The supervised model is not introduced merely to
show that volume control can regularize a hidden layer. It is a test of
whether the learning dynamics of the isolated implicit-EM site persist under
supervised composition.

\subsection{Volume Control and Collapse}
Classical mixture models motivate the distinction between local mixture
health and optimizer conditioning. Mixture likelihoods can develop singular
components, nearly hard assignments can leave components with little chance
of recovery, and diffuse responsibilities can slow specialization
\citep{redner1984mixture,mclachlan2000finite}. In the neural setting, the
corresponding at-site failures are dead components, collapsed variance,
redundant features, and weakly calibrated responsibilities.

The variance and decorrelation terms used here play the local role of volume
control. They are also related to redundancy-reduction methods in
self-supervised learning, including Barlow Twins and VICReg
\citep{zbontar2021barlow,bardes2022vicreg}. Those methods were developed for
representation learning under invariance objectives. Here the same structural
motif is attached to an intermediate EM site. One contribution of the present
paper is to show that this local repair transfers into a supervised network
even when the optimizer signature of the isolated site does not.

\subsection{Composition, Local Learning, and Credit Assignment}
The failure mode studied here is compositional. A local EM site may create a
responsibility-gradient path, but ordinary backpropagation also pulls foreign
gradients through learned maps before they reach earlier parameters. This
places the work near methods that protect or decouple local objectives.

Deep belief networks and greedy layer-wise pretraining showed that useful
representations can be learned by protected local objectives
\citep{hinton2006fast}. Later work on greedy layer-wise learning
\citep{belilovsky2019greedy}, decoupled neural interfaces and synthetic
gradients \citep{jaderberg2017decoupled}, and Forward-Forward
\citep{hinton2022forward} also explores alternatives to fully end-to-end
credit assignment. Auxiliary classifiers and deeply supervised networks
provide a supervised route for injecting intermediate losses into deep models
\citep{szegedy2015going,lee2015deeply}.

Our contribution is not a new local-learning algorithm. It is a mechanism for
why a protected local objective can preserve optimizer structure that
ordinary dense backpropagation may obscure. In the implicit-EM setting, the
protected object is the responsibility-gradient path. If it reaches private
component parameters directly, the local LSE bound controls the update. If
the same parameters are trained mainly through a dense supervised corridor,
the local bound no longer governs the effective dynamics.

\subsection{Auxiliary Losses, Dominance, and Interference}
Training with both a main CE loss and a local auxiliary EM loss places the
work near auxiliary-loss and multi-task learning. PCGrad
\citep{yu2020pcgrad} modifies conflicting task gradients, GradNorm
\citep{chen2018gradnorm} balances task weights, and gradient starvation
\citep{pezeshki2021gradient} studies one signal dominating another. These
methods address practical optimization conflicts among multiple training
signals.

The mechanism measured here is dominance rather than direct opposition. The
cosine between the CE and local-EM gradients at the intermediate distance
coordinates is near zero, not strongly negative. The EM path is diluted by a
larger foreign path rather than cancelled by an opposing one. This is why the
gradient ratio, not a conflict metric, is the central diagnostic for whether
the EM-like optimizer signature is visible at $W_1$.

\subsection{Generative and Discriminative Training}
The generative--discriminative distinction \citep{ng2002discriminative} is a
natural high-level explanation for why the isolated LSE model and the
supervised classifier behave differently. The present result is compatible
with that distinction but more specific. In the causal experiment, the data,
architecture, labels, and local site are held fixed while the route by which
cross-entropy reaches $W_1$ is changed. Cutting the CE corridor restores the
single-site optimizer signature; increasing the EM weight until the local
path dominates restores the same behavior without cutting the graph.

Thus the claim is not simply that generative and discriminative objectives
differ. The paper identifies the corridor through which the discriminative
objective reaches the local EM site and shows when that corridor controls the
effective update.

\subsection{Loss Landscapes and Basin Structure}
The loss-landscape literature provides useful future diagnostics for this
setting. Linear mode connectivity shows that networks trained in apparently
different basins can often be connected by low-loss paths
\citep{frankle2020linear}. Permutation invariance is a central complication:
component or neuron relabeling can create artificial barriers unless models
are aligned before interpolation \citep{entezari2022role,ainsworth2023git}.
Linear probes are a first diagnostic for representation alignment
\citep{alain2016understanding}.

These tools are relevant because EM-dominated and CE-dominated regimes can
produce representations with different task alignment and different local
geometry. A basin analysis would ask whether discriminative fine-tuning
refines the EM solution or moves to a distinct region after hidden-unit
alignment. That analysis is outside the present evidence. The central
evidence here is the stop-gradient and dominance intervention.

\subsection{Scope and Positioning}
This paper continues a specific line of inquiry. The attention-gradient work
of \citet{agarwal2025gradient} showed that cross-entropy training of
attention has EM-like local dynamics, while explicitly treating the
training-time EM correspondence as structural. The implicit-EM identity
\citep{oursland2025implicitem} identified the exact local algebra behind
responsibility gradients in distance-based LSE objectives. The decoder-free
study \citep{oursland2026decoderfree} tested the unsupervised consequences of
that local identity and revealed the optimizer signature that motivates the
present paper.

The current contribution is a boundary result. It is not a general theory of
deep-network optimization, Hessian spectra, or generalization. It concerns
log-sum-exp and softmax sites, the responsibility gradients those sites
create, and the corridors through which those gradients reach parameters.
Within that scope, the paper separates three properties that can otherwise be
conflated: existence of a local responsibility-gradient interface, local
volume-controlled mixture health, and EM-like optimizer dynamics at the
parameters.
